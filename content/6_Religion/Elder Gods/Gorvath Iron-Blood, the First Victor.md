**Gorvath Iron-Blood**, called the Proving Flame, is the [[Elder Gods|Elder God]] credited by orc tradition with shaping the first orcs, and the source of the doctrine that still governs orc society, the Faith of the Forge.

# Description

Gorvath is described as a towering, orcish figure, broader and more heavily scarred than any mortal frame could survive carrying. His hide is the black-red of iron fresh from the coals, and his hands are described as perpetually unfinished, skin healing over in uneven plates between one sighting and the next, as though he remains under his own hammer along with everything he has made. He carries no single weapon; smiths who claim to have glimpsed him describe whatever blade or tool lies nearest, always broken, always mid-repair. Where his voice is described at all, it is compared to metal cooling: a long, ticking groan, understood more than heard.

# Personality

Gorvath is stern and exacting, and unmoved by suffering that follows a fight actually fought rather than avoided. He is not, however, indifferent to what becomes of his creations. Clergy and myth alike hold that his harshness is deliberate rather than neglect: having made his children incomplete on purpose, he treats every hardship set in their path as an unfinished part of that same shaping, and continued attention from him, however hard, is read as proof of his regard rather than its absence. His open contempt is reserved for stagnation, the refusal to keep testing oneself, and for those who claim strength without ever having risked losing it. Orc tradition holds that no one earns his notice by avoiding a fight, only by having actually risked losing one.

# Origins and the Shaping of Orcs

Orc tradition traces the shaping of the first orcs to the age that followed the retreat of the world's floodwaters, remembered elsewhere as the [[Age of Orcs]]. Gorvath found the newly exposed coasts and highlands raw and unclaimed, and rather than fill them with beings suited to comfort, he struck the cooling earth with his hammer and called the first orcs up from the scars it left behind. Each rose unfinished: strong, but flawed by design, so that the shaping might never be considered complete. Gorvath's decree is recorded as: "Only those who prove their strength deserve to endure. All else must be broken, or remade."

Read on its own, the decree sounds like contempt for the weak. Orc tradition insists otherwise: a world scoured empty by flood and long divine absence had no patience for the unready, and Gorvath's choice was to arm his children for that world rather than shelter them from it. He is remembered as having remained among the first orcs longer than the age's other powers stayed with anyone, teaching the first forge-fires personally and standing over the first hunts before withdrawing to let his children prove what he had built into them. From this inheritance, orcs regard themselves not as a chosen people but as a people under ongoing test, one their god is said to have set them to only after seeing them through its earliest and most dangerous stage himself.

# Core Beliefs of the Faith

Orc society shaped by Gorvath's doctrine is organized around a martial meritocracy. Rank is earned through combat, leadership, or achievement rather than inherited through birth. Defeat is expected and not considered shameful; only stagnation, the refusal to improve, is condemned. War, duels, and trials carry religious as well as political weight, and leadership is treated as conditional: any leader may be challenged, and a loss is taken as proof of unworthiness. Clergy read this structure less as cruelty than as inheritance: a god who forged his children incomplete on purpose so that they would never stop needing each other's strength, nor stop earning his.

# The Forging Doctrine

Central to worship is the belief that every orc carries an "Inner Ore," a potential that must be refined through hardship. Priests teach the process in four stages: battle serves as the hammer, pain as the fire, failure as the folding of the metal, and victory as the sharpening of the blade. A life without struggle is considered spiritually unforged.

# Clergy: The Ironbound

Gorvath's priests are warrior-smiths known as the Ironbound. They judge duels and trials, reforge the weapons of fallen warriors into relics, and rule on whether a death was worthy or wasted. Orthodox Ironbound draw a line between this severity and cruelty for its own sake: a trial is meant to leave someone able to survive the next one, not simply to remove them. Clergy who blur that line, provoking conflict a hold has no hope of surviving or declaring entire bloodlines unworthy outright, are treated as an excess rather than the doctrine's intent, though orc history has not always been kind in keeping the two apart.

# Rituals and Practices

Young orcs undergo a ritual battle called the First Scar, intended to leave their first real wound as proof that their forging has begun. A defeated warrior may undergo the Ash Trial, returning from exile stronger or not returning at all. The Reforging is the practice of melting down the weapons of the fallen and redistributing the metal, symbolizing that strength never dies, only changes hands.

A death judged worthy by the Ironbound is not mourned in the manner of other peoples; it is escorted. Funeral rites for such a death treat it as this life's final trial completed, and orc communities speak of it plainly, closer to a boast than a lament, trusting that the fallen has passed into the [[Hall of the Worthy]], Gorvath's own afterlife, where the same trials continue as celebration rather than test.

# Internal Conflict

Orc interpretation of Gorvath's doctrine is not uniform. The Pure Iron hold that only constant war proves worth, and act as aggressive expansionists. The Tempered Path teach that restraint and discipline are the higher forms of strength. The Ashborn are a radical sect holding that most orcs are failures who must be culled. These divisions have produced lasting political tension, civil wars, and ideological conflict within orc society.

The clearest instance came centuries later, when the war-leader [[Kharzug One-Eye]] preached that Gorvath's own afterlife had grown soft with the unworthy and called the [[Unbroken Host]] to a war of purification he named the Final Proving. No faction achieved the victory it sought; the fighting only left the Host too weak to hold its own lands, and it was overtaken by monsters and rival powers in the aftermath. Whether Kharzug spoke for Gorvath or only for himself remains argued among the sects to this day. Orthodox Ironbound point to the outcome as its own answer: a doctrine meant to strengthen a people had been turned toward destroying it, an outcome no reading of the Forging Doctrine was ever meant to permit.

# Divine Behavior

Gorvath is not held to answer prayers for mercy or safety, and Ironbound teaching holds that this refusal is itself the clearest evidence of his regard: a god who wanted his children merely comfortable would not have made them incomplete to begin with. He is said to empower those who fight against overwhelming odds, to break the weapons of those he deems unworthy, and to send visions before major battles that offer no clear answers. Among his worshippers, his blessing is commonly understood to arrive in the form of a harder challenge, one orc tradition holds falls hardest on those he watches most closely.

Tradition draws a firm line, however, between a hard trial and true annihilation. Several old accounts describe Gorvath acting, directly or through omen, when a hold or people faced outright extinction rather than ordinary defeat, intervention read as proof that the Proving Flame tests his children to make them stronger, not to see them erased. That same distinction is said to carry into the [[Hall of the Worthy]], the afterlife Gorvath keeps for orcs who die well: unlike the distant god who tests the living, he is said to walk the Hall itself in mortal guise, sitting beside an unremarkable warrior whose spirit impressed him or challenging a champion directly at a decisive moment. No one is told in advance when they have met him, and it is this presence among the dead, more than anything demanded of the living, that orc tradition points to when asked why a god who offers so little comfort in life is still remembered as a father.
