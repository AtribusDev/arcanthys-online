The **[[Age of Shaping]]** is the earliest era described in any surviving tradition, known almost entirely through myth, religious epic, and inherited doctrine rather than verifiable record. No firsthand account of the age has ever been found, and what is known today survives only through retellings passed down across countless generations.

# The Legend

No single scripture preserves the tale whole. What survives is a mosaic of hymns, court epics, and oral retellings drawn from a dozen traditions, agreeing on shape more often than on detail. Even among the devout, it is repeated and taught as legend, not as a literal chronicle of events. Compiled together, it is most often told something like this:

> Before the First Gods, before the Fell Lords, before a single mortal walked upon the land, there was only a barren world drifting at the edge of an empty cosmos.
>
> [[Arcanum (Creator God of Myth)|Arcanum]] found it. Moved by its unrealized potential, he gave his own essence so that it might live, and where his light fell the world took shape: seas rose, mountains stood, and creation found its laws.
>
> Having given all he could, Arcanum charged the [[The First Gods|First Gods]] to finish what he began. They walked the young world and filled it with the quiet order of nature, with beast and root and season, and for a time creation rested content beneath their care.
>
> Then came others from beyond, gods unbound by the First Gods' order. Where the First Gods had shaped a world of instinct, these new gods, the ones later remembered as the [[The Fell Lords|Fell Lords]], shaped something new: mortals who could think, and feel, and choose for themselves.
>
> The Fell Lords looked upon these thinking children as their own, creations to be guided, governed, and bound to divine law so that their emerging power might be turned toward purpose. The First Gods looked upon the same children and saw beings meant to be free, for Arcanum had given life freely and without chains, and any hand that sought to bind it defied him. Neither would yield, and so the gods went to war over the fate of the world's newest children.

*(The verses describing the war itself diverge most between the surviving traditions. What follows reflects the ending most commonly repeated.)*

> Mortals, it is said, did not merely watch this war unfold. They prayed, and knelt, and believed, and through that belief the First Gods found a strength no god had wielded before. One by one the Fell Lords fell before it, until at last they were bound within Arcanthys itself, sealed away by the hand of their rivals.
>
> Their victory won, the First Gods withdrew from the world they had saved, and have not been seen upon it since.

This withdrawal is held to mark the end of the Age of Shaping, and the beginning of the [[Age of the Baneful]].

# Modern Understanding

Every surviving version of the legend descends from faiths that trace themselves back to the First Gods, a point scholars note but rarely treat as more than an accident of which traditions happened to endure and keep records.

The claim that mortal belief itself turned the tide of the war is debated at length. Some read it plainly, as an early demonstration of the same link between faith and divine power still observed today. Others treat it as a later addition, shaped by generations of clergy with an obvious interest in describing prayer as something capable of winning wars. Both readings are taught side by side in most modern seminaries, and neither has displaced the other.

Equally debated is what became of the First Gods after their victory. The legend states plainly that they withdrew and were never seen again, but offers no account of where they went or in what state they remain. Proposed explanations range from a wound too grievous to recover from, to a deliberate choice to stay apart so as not to risk repeating the war's cost, to the more sceptical view that they simply could not return even if they wished to. No surviving evidence favours any one of these over the others.

Nor have the First Gods themselves settled the matter. What remains of their will still reaches Arcanthys only through prophecy, cleric, and omen, faint and infrequent by nature, and in all the ages since their withdrawal none of it is recorded to have confirmed or corrected a single detail of the tale. Whether this silence reflects gods too diminished to speak more plainly, a deliberate choice to let the legend stand as taught, or simply a question that prophecy has never been asked in the right way, is debated with as much conviction as the legend itself.

What can be said with more confidence is that the legend was never meant to function as a chronicle in the modern sense. It is scripture, composed to be recited and believed rather than verified, and most who study the age treat it accordingly: less as a puzzle to be solved than as a window into what the faiths that carried it forward wanted remembered, and why.
